Why these six

Light. Brain serotonin turnover in healthy people rises and falls with the amount of sunlight on the day of measurement — the effect is fast, and it's the reason bright-light therapy works for seasonal depression. Ten minutes outdoors beats an hour indoors: an overcast morning outside is still ten to fifty times brighter than your kitchen.

Move. Rhythmic aerobic movement raises tryptophan availability to the brain and increases firing in serotonergic neurons. Trial evidence for exercise against depression is among the strongest of anything on this list. Twenty minutes, most days, at a pace where you could still talk.

Sleep. A regular wake time — the same one on Saturday — predicts mood better than total hours slept. It's the anchor the other five hang off; light hits differently when your clock knows what time it is.

People. One real exchange with someone who knows you. Not a like, not a broadcast, not a reply-all. Loneliness moves mood on the scale of a major health risk, and the fix is specific: contact where you're a person, not an audience.

Savor. Writing down one good thing, in detail, is the smallest intervention with repeatedly measured effects on mood — modest each time, cumulative over weeks. The detail is the active ingredient; "nice day" does nothing.

Finish. One small thing completed, start to end. Behavioral activation — doing before feeling like it — is a first-line depression treatment on its own, and finishing is the part that pays.

Sources worth your time: Lambert et al., Lancet 2002 (sunlight & serotonin turnover) · Young, J Psychiatry Neurosci 2007 (raising serotonin without drugs) · Noetel et al., BMJ 2024 (exercise for depression) · Cuijpers et al. (behavioral activation).

Honest note. "Serotonin" is shorthand here. Nothing on this page measures your neurochemistry, and no app can. What these six have in common is that they're the daily behaviors with the most consistent evidence behind them for mood — and that they work by raising a floor slowly, not by spiking anything. If your floor stays down for weeks, or you're thinking about hurting yourself, that's a doctor's job and not an app's. In the US you can call or text 988.
